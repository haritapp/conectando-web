When your event ends, if the reception is held somewhere far away, or if there is a long gap before it starts, many people simply leave. Some go off to have dinner elsewhere. Not everyone, but honestly, quite a lot of people go home.

## The venue decides your reception

The basic rule is to hold the reception right next to your event venue and start it without a gap. When you plan the reception, decide the location and budget first.

The best options are one of these two:

- A venue with restaurants in the same building. For example, Shibuya Hikarie has event halls and restaurants in one building.
- Book two rooms, and turn one of them into the reception space partway through the event.

If you have room in your budget, a hotel is also a good choice. It costs more, but hotels can handle special meals such as vegan menus, which makes things much easier.

## A uniquely Japanese venue can be worth the distance

There is one exception: uniquely Japanese places such as temples or Noh theaters. I have held receptions at a temple and at a Noh theater myself. Guests from overseas love these places, and they give Japanese attendees something to talk about too. They are worth choosing even if they are away from your event venue. The risk is that some people leave while moving between the two.

## After the venue, what matters most is someone who connects people

Japanese attendees, including VIPs, worry about the language barrier. If you do nothing, Japanese and overseas guests will split into completely separate groups at the reception.

So put someone in place who connects people in both languages. In my case, I did this myself as the organizer: I went around introducing attendees to each other, working in both English and Japanese. A student volunteer can also play this role.
